1. Which design do I need?

Ask yourself two questions. (a) Is my main outcome a number (blood pressure, a score) or a yes/no (cured, anaemic)? (b) Am I comparing two separate groups, measuring the same people twice, or describing one group?

Your questionOutcomeGroupsTab to choose
Does drug A lower blood pressure more than drug B?NumberTwo separate groupsCompare two means
Is the cure rate higher with treatment A than B?Yes / noTwo separate groupsCompare two proportions
What percentage of students in my college are anaemic?Yes / noOne group (a survey)Estimate a prevalence
Did body weight change after the diet programme?NumberSame people, before and afterPaired means
Is BMI related to cholesterol?Two numbersOne groupCorrelation
Not covered here: survival or time-to-event outcomes, three or more groups, repeated measurements over time, non-inferiority or equivalence trials, cluster or multi-centre designs, and diagnostic-accuracy studies. These need different formulas. Please ask a statistician before you finalise.

2. The four boxes on every tab

The same four settings appear below your design-specific inputs. The last column shows how each setting changes the sample size in a typical example (two means, SD 12, difference 6), where the baseline is 64 per group.

BoxWhat it meansWhat to pickEffect on sample size
Significance level (alpha)The chance of a false alarm: concluding there is an effect when there is none.0.05 almost always. Use 0.01 only if your supervisor or the field requires stricter evidence.0.01 needs more (64 → 96).
PowerThe chance your study will detect a real effect of the size you entered. Low power means a real effect can be missed.80% is the usual minimum. Choose 90% for important or hard-to-repeat studies.90% needs more (64 → 86).
Test: two-sided or one-sidedTwo-sided allows the effect to go either way. One-sided assumes it can only go one way.Two-sided unless you can justify, before the study, that the opposite direction is impossible or irrelevant. The Power and Test boxes disappear on the prevalence tab, because that design estimates a value with a margin of error and does not test a hypothesis.

3. The boxes for each design

Compare two means

BoxWhat it meansExample
Standard deviation of the outcomeHow much the measurement varies from person to person. A bigger spread needs a bigger sample.Systolic BP in similar patients has an SD of about 12 mmHg.
Smallest difference worth detectingThe smallest gap between the groups that would matter to a doctor or patient. It is not the difference you hope to see.A 6 mmHg fall is clinically meaningful.
Allocation ratioParticipants in group 2 for every one in group 1. Leave at 1 for equal groups.2 means two controls for each case.

Compare two proportions

BoxWhat it meansExample
Expected proportion, group 1 and group 2The share of people with the outcome in each group, written as a decimal between 0 and 1. 70% is 0.70, not 70. They must differ, and their gap is the effect you want to detect.Cure rate 0.70 with the new treatment versus 0.50 with the standard one.
Allocation ratioAs above.Keep at 1 unless you plan unequal groups.

Estimate a prevalence

BoxWhat it meansExample
Expected prevalenceYour best guess of the true proportion, as a decimal, from earlier studies. If you truly have no idea, use 0.5: it gives the largest, safest sample.Earlier surveys found about 30% anaemia, so 0.30.
Absolute precisionHow far your estimate may stray from the truth, in percentage points, as a decimal. Smaller means a bigger sample.0.05 means plus or minus 5 percentage points (25% to 35%).

4. Where do my numbers come from?

The calculator does the arithmetic. The assumptions are yours, and your protocol should say where they came from.

  • A pilot study of 20 to 30 participants gives a rough SD or proportion. Do not use the pilot's effect directly, because small pilots overestimate it.
  • Published papers on the same outcome in a similar population. Search for the same measure and take the SD or proportion reported. If a paper gives a standard error (SE), the SD is SE × √n. If it gives a 95% confidence interval for a mean, SD ≈ √n × (upper − lower) / 3.92.
  • The smallest clinically important difference comes from clinical judgement or published guidelines, not from a statistics rule. Ask your supervisor: "What is the smallest change that would change practice?"
  • No information at all? For a prevalence use 0.5. For two means, Cohen's rule of thumb (a difference of 0.2, 0.5 or 0.8 SDs for small, medium or large) is a last resort and a weak justification. Say so honestly.

Two-sided → one-sided51 per groupCheaper, but only if you can defend it.

7. Common mistakes

  • Choosing the effect size to get a small sample. Enter the smallest difference that matters clinically, not the one that makes the number comfortable.
  • Typing percentages as whole numbers. Proportions are decimals: 0.70, not 70. The calculator will warn you.
  • Using the SE instead of the SD. The SE is much smaller and will make your sample far too small.
  • Forgetting dropout. Always allow for it, then recruit the "with dropout" total.
  • Mixing up precision. "Plus or minus 5%" in a prevalence study means 5 percentage points (0.05), not 5% of the estimate.
  • Only sizing for one outcome. Calculate for your primary outcome. If you plan many subgroup or secondary tests, discuss this with your statistician.
  • Treating the result as exact. It is only as reliable as your assumptions. Test a few plausible values to see how sensitive it is.

8. Putting it in your thesis or protocol

  • Use the methods sentence the calculator writes, and edit it to match your study.
  • State where each assumption came from, with the paper or pilot study cited.
  • Name the method: normal-approximation formulas (Chow, Shao and Wang; Fleiss; Lwanga and Lemeshow). Mention the calculator if you used it, and keep a screenshot of your inputs.
  • Report both the calculated size and the recruited size with dropout.

9. Glossary

AlphaThe false-alarm rate you accept. Usually 0.05.
PowerThe chance of detecting a real effect. Usually at least 80%.
Standard deviation (SD)A measure of how spread out the measurements are.
Effect size / difference worth detectingHow big a difference or relationship you want to be able to find.
Precision (margin of error)How close an estimate should be to the truth in a survey.
Allocation ratioThe relative size of group 2 compared with group 1.
DropoutParticipants who leave or do not respond.
